Picking the mirror that moves single atoms

An optical tweezer traps a single neutral atom in a tightly focused laser beam. To move the atom, you move the beam — so a galvanometer mirror does the steering, and the mirror's stability becomes the atom's stability. Datasheets do not answer that question, so I built a bench to measure it: a quadrant photodiode at the end of a 300 mm propagation path, turning where the spot lands into a voltage I could log.

I calibrated voltage-to-angle for three candidates, then characterized each one with the mirror off, holding at DC, and under square-wave drive at 100 Hz, 1 kHz and 10 kHz. A single-element photodiode ran in parallel to confirm the differences were real and not artifacts of the quadrant-photodiode processing.

Thorlabs QS15Y~50 µrad~600 µrad~30 ms

Overshoot and settling measured on a 10 Hz, 20 mrad peak-to-peak square step.

The Cambridge unit settles more than an order of magnitude faster than anything else in the table, which makes it the obvious pick until you look at the other two columns — 150 µrad of jitter and 4 mrad of overshoot would throw an atom out of the trap long before the speed helped. I selected the Scanlab, then built and aligned the optical rig around it (fiber collimation, cabling, heat sinking) and integrated it for transport of 500 µm or more in the science plane, using the objective's 41.3 mrad ↔ 400 µm mapping to convert commanded angle into real displacement.

Two things I did not expect. A signal generator was injecting a 24 kHz spur into the drive, which vanished once I moved to a DAC. And ramping the steps rather than stepping hard cut jitter from roughly 900 to 500 µrad at 100 Hz — but each added step lengthens the ramp, and the firmware froze past about 660 of them, so the operating envelope is a genuine trade rather than a setting to max out.

I led the galvo characterization subproject, defined the test protocols, and drove the selection. Supported by an HCRP award, summer 2025.

A satellite solar array that moves without a motor

HUCSat is a 2U CubeSat — about the size of two stacked Rubik's cubes — selected by NASA's CubeSat Launch Initiative. Its payload asks a narrow question: can you point a solar panel in space using an alloy instead of a mechanism?

Nitinol is a nickel-titanium alloy that can be trained to a shape and returns to it when heated. The payload puts four nitinol coils around a solar platform, one per edge, and heats them selectively. Heating them unevenly makes the contraction asymmetric, which rotates the platform toward the sun. There is no motor and no gearbox in the loop, and power is drawn only in the moments when it is actually moving.

I designed the nitinol spring actuator system in SolidWorks and supported prototype testing — furnace heat-cycling to see whether the trained shape held up under repeated actuation — then documented the results. We characterized the payload in-house before delivering it to NASA.

The satellite deployed from the International Space Station in April 2026.

Cheap mass and scarce missiles

Harvard Undergraduate Foreign Policy Initiative · Policy Chair · 2026

A 37-page brief and executive deck written for Anduril on integrating thousands of cheap, mass-producible strike vehicles with scarce high-end missiles.

The argument is integration rather than substitution. The framework weighs cost-exchange ratios, production capacity, feedstocks, logistics, and survivability under electronic warfare — the constraints that decide what can actually be built, fielded and replaced, as opposed to what performs best on paper.
