Technical details

Picking the mirror that moves single atoms

An optical tweezer traps a single neutral atom in a tightly focused laser beam. To move the atom, you move the beam — which means a galvanometer mirror is doing the steering, and its stability becomes the atom's stability. Datasheets do not tell you what you need to know here, so I built a bench to measure it.

A position-sensing detector in the beam path turns where the spot lands into a voltage. I wrote Python drivers to generate the galvo waveforms, drive the mirrors through repeated moves, and compute stability metrics from the logged data: how far the beam drifts when it is supposed to be holding still, and how long it takes to settle after a step.

I characterized three mirrors that way. The one I selected holds to under 15 µrad of DC jitter with roughly 5 ms step-settling — good enough for stable atom transport. I then built and aligned the optical rig around it (fiber collimation, cabling, heat sinking) and integrated the galvo for transport of 500 µm or more in the science plane.

I led the galvo characterization subproject, defined the test protocols, and drove the mirror selection. Supported by an HCRP award, summer 2025.

A satellite solar array that moves without a motor

HUCSat is a 2U CubeSat — about the size of two stacked Rubik's cubes — selected by NASA's CubeSat Launch Initiative. Its payload asks a narrow question: can you point a solar panel in space using an alloy instead of a mechanism?

Nitinol is a nickel-titanium alloy that can be trained to a shape and returns to it when heated. The payload puts four nitinol coils around a solar platform, one per edge, and heats them selectively. Heating them unevenly makes the contraction asymmetric, which rotates the platform toward the sun. There is no motor and no gearbox in the loop, and power is drawn only in the moments when it is actually moving.

I designed the nitinol spring actuator system in SolidWorks and supported prototype testing — furnace heat-cycling to see whether the trained shape held up under repeated actuation — then documented the results. We characterized the payload in-house before delivering it to NASA.

The satellite deployed from the International Space Station in April 2026.
